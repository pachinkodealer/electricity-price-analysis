For decades, US retail electricity tracked the price of natural gas. Around 2020 that link broke — fuel got cheap again, but bills kept climbing — and the timing lines up with the first sustained growth in US power demand in two decades: the AI datacenter buildout.

Motivation

The question

From 2014 to 2020, average US electricity prices were essentially flat. Since 2020 they have risen 48%.

A jump like that usually has a simple cause: the fuel that runs power plants got more expensive, or general inflation lifted everything. So — is this just fuel and inflation, or is something genuinely new driving it?

Findings
1
Electricity ratchets. It follows gas up with a ~6-month lag (r = 0.53) but did not retrace when gas gave back its entire 2022 spike.
2
A persistent ~2.6 pts/yr of electricity inflation since 2023 is unexplained by fuel or CPI — coinciding with the datacenter-driven return of demand growth.
3
Elevated, not unprecedented. Prior residual episodes (2005–09) decayed within a couple of years; this one hasn't — and its driver is still growing.
Stated limitations

The residual is “not gas, not CPI” — it bundles datacenter demand with grid capex, plant retirements, and electrification. It is not a clean causal estimate.

Overlapping YoY series are heavily autocorrelated (Durbin–Watson ≈ 0.2): a descriptive decomposition, not formal inference.

Conclusions

The link between fuel and your bill is broken. In the shale decade, cheap gas meant flat prices; that cool-off is absent today.

Whatever replaced fuel as the driver is growing, not fading. The honest claim isn't “unprecedented” — it's elevated, persistent, and attached to demand that keeps rising.

Not everyone uses AI.
But everyone pays an electric bill.
